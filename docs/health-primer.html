Health Metrics Primer

Every health number dashboardify works out from the strap: what it means for your body, how it is calculated step by step, the research behind it, and a small worked example.

How to read this

The strap sends six raw streams. Everything else on the Health page is arithmetic on top of them. This page follows that arithmetic as it stands on main at 12 Nov 2026, up to decoding awake together with the sleep stages (H15). It lives in the repo as docs/health-primer.html and changes with the code: a test recomputes the worked examples.

One rule runs through the whole system. Anything that compares you with "normal" compares you with your own recent history, never with population averages. A resting heart rate of 58 is neither good nor bad. It is good if you are usually at 62 and bad if you are usually at 52. VO2max is the one exception, because it is a comparison with other people by definition.

Six ideas to know first

1. Two nerves steer your heart

Your heart beats on its own, but two branches of the autonomic nervous system adjust the pace. The sympathetic branch ("fight or flight") speeds it up and acts over several seconds. The parasympathetic branch ("rest and digest"), working through the vagus nerve, slows it down and acts within a single beat. Most numbers on this page are indirect readings of the balance between the two.

2. The gap between beats matters as much as the rate

At 60 beats a minute the average gap is 1,000 ms, but real gaps wander: 1,000, 1,040, 980. That wandering is heart rate variability. More wandering at rest means the fast-acting vagus nerve is in charge, which is what a rested body looks like.

3. Median and percentile

The median is the middle value when you sort a list. Unlike the average, one wild reading barely moves it, so the code uses it wherever sensor glitches are likely. The 10th percentile is the value that 10% of readings fall below: "among your lowest, without trusting the single lowest".

4. z-score: how unusual is this for me?

A z-score says how many standard deviations a value sits from your own average.

z = \frac{\text{tonight} - \text{your mean}}{\text{your standard deviation}}

If your resting heart rate averages 52 with a standard deviation of 2, a night at 50 is z = −1. About two nights in three fall between −1 and +1.

5. Heart-rate reserve

Raw heart rate is a poor measure of effort, because 120 bpm is easy for one person and hard for another. Heart-rate reserve (HRR) rescales it: 0 at your resting rate, 1 at your maximum.

\text{HRR} = \frac{\text{HR} - \text{HR}_{\text{rest}}}{\text{HR}_{\text{max}} - \text{HR}_{\text{rest}}}

For the example person at 150 bpm: (150 − 52) ÷ (190 − 52) = 0.71, or 71% of reserve.

6. Estimates stay labelled as estimates

A strap on the arm cannot measure brain waves, oxygen uptake or energy burned. It measures heart beats and movement, and the rest is inferred. Each section ends with how far to trust the number, and the table near the end collects them.

Part one · The night

Sleep window

src/health/metrics/sleep.ts

The sleep window is the stretch from lying down to getting up. It is found from movement alone. Asleep, you change posture only now and then. Awake, your arm tilts constantly.

How it is calculated

  1. Arm angle. At rest the accelerometer feels only gravity, so its three readings say which way the strap is tilted.
    \text{angle} = \arctan\!\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}}\right)
  2. Average the angle over each 5 seconds, then take the absolute change from one 5-second step to the next.
  3. Smooth with a 5-minute rolling median, so one twitch does not count as waking up.
  4. Stillness threshold. 15 × the night's 10th-percentile change, kept between 0.13° and 0.5°. The threshold scales to how still you are on this night.
  5. Find still stretches of 30 minutes or more. Join stretches less than an hour apart, so getting up for water does not split the night.
  6. The longest joined block between 18:00 the day before and 14:00 is the sleep window.

Worked example

gravity reading x = 0.50, y = 0.50, z = 0.71 angle arctan(0.71 ÷ √(0.25 + 0.25)) = 45.1° 5 seconds later 45.3° → change 0.2° night's 10th-percentile change 0.012° threshold 15 × 0.012 = 0.18°

Any smoothed step under 0.18° counts as still. A still block from 23:38 to 03:10 and another from 03:25 to 07:20 are 15 minutes apart, so they join into one window: 23:38–07:20.

How far to trust it. The method was built and tested for the wrist. The strap sits on the bicep, which moves less, so the threshold may need tuning. Reading in bed while very still can be counted as part of the window; the next step catches most of that.

Sources: van Hees 2015 (arm angle from a wrist accelerometer); van Hees 2018 (the "HDCZA" window finder used in the GGIR package).

Asleep or awake

src/health/metrics/sleep-epochs.ts

Lying still is not sleeping. Inside the window, the night is cut into 30-second epochs, the unit sleep labs use, and each is scored asleep or awake. This happens in two passes: simple rules first, which find when you fell asleep, then the sleep-stage model, which decides awake for the rest of the night. The heart helps in both. Each arousal from sleep comes with a burst of sympathetic activity that lifts heart rate for a few beats to a minute, even if you do not move.

How it is calculated

First pass: rules. An epoch is awake if any one of these is true:

  • Posture change: the arm angle moved more than 5° between 5-second steps.
  • Arousal: heart rate is 8 bpm or more above the median of the surrounding 10 minutes.
  • Lying awake: heart rate is 20% or more above the night's sleeping level (its 10th percentile). REM sleep also raises heart rate, but typically by 5–10%, so it stays under this line.
  • No heart rate: the strap was off or lost contact.

Then one clean-up pass: a lone awake epoch between two asleep ones becomes asleep, and the reverse. One fast beat is not waking up. Sleep onset is the start of the first 10 unbroken minutes of sleep in this scoring.

Second pass: the stage model. After onset the rules' verdict is no longer final. Scored one epoch at a time, a minute of fast beats during REM looked like an awakening: on 3–11 Nov the rules alone gave 21–39 awakenings a night, and 6–13 times a night the pattern REM, awake, REM. The stage model weighs each epoch against its neighbours instead, so a blip inside steady REM stays REM, while a long stretch of high heart rate and movement is still awake. The rules' findings (an arousal, and how big) go in as evidence.

From the result come the standard sleep-lab measures:

MeasureDefinition
Sleep onsetThe start of the first 10 unbroken minutes of sleep (first pass)
LatencyStart of the window to onset: how long you took to fall asleep
WASO"Wake after sleep onset": awake minutes after onset
AwakeningsSeparate awake runs of a minute or more after onset
EfficiencyMinutes asleep ÷ minutes in the window

Worked example

night's sleeping HR (10th percentile) 50 bpm lying-awake line 1.2 × 50 = 60 bpm epoch A HR 61 → awake (over 60) epoch B HR 58, local median 49 → awake (9 above, arousal) epoch C HR 55, local median 52, still → asleep Before onset those verdicts stand. After onset, A and B are "arousal" cues for the stage model, which has the last word. window 23:38–07:20 = 462 min latency 12 min → onset 23:50 asleep 410 min WASO 462 − 12 − 410 = 40 min efficiency 410 ÷ 462 = 88.7%

How far to trust it. Total sleep time from movement and heart rate is usually within tens of minutes of a sleep lab for healthy sleepers. Quiet wakefulness with a calm heart is the usual miss. Against the sleep lab, the model finds about 4 in 10 awake epochs and wrongly calls 1 in 20 sleeping epochs awake. Those errors pull in opposite directions: time awake after onset came to 29 minutes a night against the lab's 21. Most of the lab's awakenings last a minute or less, and those are rarely seen.

Sources: Webster 1982 (rescoring lone epochs); van Hees 2015 (the 5° posture change).

Sleep stages

src/health/metrics/sleep-features.ts · sleep-stages.ts · stage-params.json · src/health/feedback/stage-em.ts · stage-fit.ts · src/health/fit-stages.ts

Sleep is not one state. Through the night you cycle, roughly every 90 minutes, through three kinds.

StageWhat it doesWhat the body shows
LightThe bulk of the night; the transition stateMiddling everything
DeepPhysical repair, growth hormone, clearing the brain. Mostly in the first half.Vagus in charge: steady heart, high variability, very regular breathing, almost no movement
REMDreaming, memory, mood. Mostly in the second half.Autonomic instability: higher, jumpy heart rate, lower variability, irregular breathing, body paralysed

A sleep lab tells them apart from brain waves. The strap cannot, so the stages are inferred from how the heart, lungs and arm behave. This is the least certain number on the page.

Step 1: describe each epoch

Each epoch gets seven features. The first four are z-scores against the night's own asleep epochs, since stages are relative to your night, not to fixed numbers.

Step 2: a hidden Markov model

A hidden Markov model (HMM) is a way to guess a hidden sequence (the stages) from a visible one (the features). It has two parts.

  • Emissions: for each stage, how likely are these features? Each z-scored feature is scored against a bell curve for that stage. A missing feature adds nothing. The heart-rate cue and movement are scored from tables of how often each happens in each stage.
    \ln P(z \mid \text{stage}) = -\frac{(z - \mu_{\text{stage}})^2}{2\sigma_{\text{stage}}^2} - \ln \sigma_{\text{stage}}
  • Transitions: given the stage now, how likely is each stage 30 seconds later? Stages are sticky: light stays light about 95% of the time. There is one transition table before onset and one per 30 minutes since, which is how "deep early, REM late" gets in.

The Viterbi algorithm then finds the single most likely path through the whole night. It walks forward keeping, for each stage, the best-scoring route that ends there, then traces back from the best ending. Because stages are sticky, one odd epoch does not flip the stage. Sustained evidence does.

Awake is the fourth state. Before sleep onset the rules decide it. After onset it is decoded like the others. In the lab data an awake epoch moves far more (44% of them, against 8% in light sleep) and shows a heart-rate cue more often (28%, against 5%). Big jumps are counted in the cue table and kept out of the bell curve: with one curve over both, its thin tail would decide what a heart rate of 150 means. Nobody has measured how HRV, breathing regularity or DFA look when awake on this strap, so awake borrows light's levels for those.

Three rules are fixed. Before onset, awake is whatever the first pass said. Sleep starts in light, never straight into deep or REM. REM cannot start in the first 45 minutes: the heart is still settling then and can look like REM, and REM that early is a sign of narcolepsy rather than healthy sleep.

Between the sleep stages, movement and the cue count only when they happen. Stillness is normal in every sleep stage, and scoring hundreds of still epochs would add up to a steady pull toward deep. Awake is the exception: lying still with a calm heart is real evidence against being awake, so for awake it does count.

Worked example: one epoch

A still epoch three hours in, with heart rate z = +0.9, HRV z = −0.5, breathing z = −0.7 and DFA z = +0.6. Log scores with the hand-set values (higher is more likely):

HR HRV breathing DFA total light −0.74 +0.03 −0.16 −0.06 −0.93 deep −0.37 −1.10 −1.53 −0.90 −3.91 REM −0.05 +0.22 +0.22 +0.22 +0.60

REM beats light by 1.53, so on this evidence alone REM is about e1.53 ≈ 4.6 times more likely. But if the previous epoch was light, switching costs: three hours in, light→REM has a chance of 0.018 against 0.947 for staying, a penalty of ln(0.018 ÷ 0.947) = −3.95. One such epoch is not enough (1.53 < 3.95). Three in a row are (3 × 1.53 = 4.59), so a minute and a half of REM-like signals flips the path to REM.

Awake is not in the table. For this epoch it scores −1.13: light's score on HRV, breathing and DFA, −0.17 on heart rate, and −0.78 for being still with no arousal. That puts it behind REM and light, and well ahead of deep.

Fitting the stages to you

The hand-set HRV, breathing and DFA curves are guesses about an average person. fit-stages.ts replaces them with curves learned from your own nights. No night has lab labels, so it uses expectation–maximisation (EM, here called Baum–Welch), which takes turns at two steps.

  1. Expectation. With the current model, work out for every epoch the chance that it was light, deep or REM, given the whole night. Viterbi picks one best path. This step (the forward–backward algorithm) keeps the doubt instead: an epoch can be 70% light and 30% REM.
  2. Maximisation. Re-estimate each stage's mean and spread for the three features as weighted averages, where each epoch counts by its chance of being in that stage.
  3. Repeat until a round barely improves the fit (under 0.01 nats of log-likelihood).

Heart rate, movement, transitions and the time-of-night tables stay as the lab nights trained them. They are the anchor that keeps "deep" meaning deep while the other three move. The current values also count as 200 epochs of evidence (100 minutes, about one night's deep sleep), so one odd night can move a stage about halfway at most, and a week outweighs it five to one.

A fit is saved only if it passes every guardrail:

  • 5 or more nights, about 4,000 epochs, and no night more than a fifth of them.
  • The order the papers give is kept: DFA α1 deep < light < REM, and HRV and breathing regularity higher in deep than in REM.
  • No spread under 0.3, and no mean moves by more than 0.5 in one go.
  • 75% or more of the nights still have a plausible split afterwards.

A saved fit gets a new params version, the same one recovery's weights use, so every stored night records which model staged it.

The hand-set values are still the ones in use. The first try, on 4–9 Nov, was rejected: it left light sleep too low on four of six nights, shifting the shares from 48 / 24 / 28 to 36 / 27 / 37 (light / deep / REM). Since awake has been decoded with the stages, a report-only run on 3–11 Nov passes, with all nine nights plausible. Nothing changes until that fit is applied.

The stage columns are recall: of the epochs the lab called deep, the share the model also called deep. κ (Cohen's kappa) is agreement beyond chance: 0 is guessing, 1 is perfect. Two human scorers reading the same lab recording reach about 0.8. Heart-rate-and-motion models usually land at 0.3–0.5, and this one is at the low end of that range. Decoding awake mostly helped REM. In the lab data 19% of REM epochs carry a heart-rate arousal, and the rules alone called those awake. A night is flagged "unusual" when light falls outside 33–61%, deep outside 2–48% or REM outside 5–51% of asleep time. The bounds are wide because the model's own error is wide.

How far to trust it. Treat one night's split as a rough sketch. A little over half of individual epochs are right. Trends over weeks ("less deep sleep since I started late dinners") are more believable than any single night, and even those rest on HRV, breathing and DFA settings that are hand-set.

DFA α1

src/health/metrics/dfa.ts

DFA α1 is one of the stage features, but it needs its own explanation. HRV asks how much the gaps between beats vary. DFA α1 asks how they vary: does a long gap tend to be followed by another long gap, or is each gap unrelated to the last?

α1PatternSeen in
about 0.5Uncorrelated: each gap is a fresh coin tossDeep sleep, very hard exercise
about 1.0"1/f": small fast wobbles riding on larger slow onesHealthy rest, REM, wake
about 1.5Random walk: each gap is the last one plus a nudgeStrongly drifting heart rate

How it is calculated

DFA stands for detrended fluctuation analysis. "α1" is the short-range version, over 4 to 16 beats.

  1. Integrate. Subtract the mean gap from every gap and keep a running total. This turns the series into a wandering line, the profile.
    y(k) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \left(RR_i - \overline{RR}\right)
  2. Box and detrend. Cut the profile into boxes of n beats. In each box, fit a straight line and measure how far the profile strays from it.
  3. Fluctuation. F(n) is the root-mean-square of that straying over all boxes.
    F(n) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\bigl(y(k) - y_n(k)\bigr)^2}
  4. Repeat for every box size from 4 to 16.
  5. Slope. Plot \ln F(n) against \ln n. The slope of the best-fit line is α1. If straying grows quickly as boxes get longer, the gaps are correlated.

Per epoch, it runs over the longest run of clean, consecutive beats in the surrounding 5 minutes and needs at least 100 beats. One dropped gap is bridged; two in a row, or a missing beat, end the run.

Worked example

Two made-up series of 300 gaps, run through dfaAlpha1:

independent gaps around 1,000 ms (coin tosses) α1 = 0.53 each gap = previous gap + a small nudge (drift) α1 = 1.60

Theory says 0.5 and 1.5. The small differences are what 300 beats of randomness give.

On six real nights (4–9 Nov), with no lab labels to check against, α1 averaged 0.76 in epochs decoded deep, 0.86 in light and 1.15 in REM, and 0.83 in the first 90 minutes against 1.01 in the last two hours. That matches the papers' direction. It correlated only −0.15 with HRV, so it adds information HRV does not have.

How far to trust it. The calculation is standard. Its weight in the stage model is hand-set until a fit to your nights passes. The check above is partly circular, since the stages it was compared with came from the same model.

Sources: Peng 1995 (the method and the 4–16 beat range); Bunde 2000 and Penzel 2003 (correlations lost in deep sleep, strong in REM); Gronwald 2020 and Rogers 2021 (the same range in exercise work; tolerance to a few dropped beats).

Sleep score

src/health/metrics/sleep-score.ts

One number from 0 to 100 answering three questions: did you get the sleep you needed, how much of your time in bed was sleep, and was your timing regular?

\text{score} = 0.6 \times \text{sufficiency} + 0.2 \times \text{efficiency} + 0.2 \times \text{consistency}

How it is calculated

  1. Need. A base of 8 hours (sleep_need_h in profile.md overrides it), plus a third of the shortfall over the last three nights, up to one extra hour. Sleep debt is real, but you do not repay it minute for minute.
  2. Sufficiency = asleep ÷ need, capped at 100.
  3. Efficiency score. 65% efficiency scores 0 and 95% scores 100, in a straight line between.
    \text{efficiency score} = 100 \times \frac{\text{efficiency} - 0.65}{0.95 - 0.65}
  4. Consistency. Compare tonight's onset and wake times with the average of the last 7 nights. Take the mean of the two differences in minutes. 0 minutes off scores 100 and 90 minutes off scores 0. It needs 3 earlier nights. Until then its weight is shared between the other two.

Worked example

last three nights asleep 450, 420, 480 min shortfall against 480 30 + 60 + 0 = 90 min need 480 + 90 ÷ 3 = 510 min sufficiency 410 ÷ 510 = 80.4 efficiency (0.8874 − 0.65) ÷ 0.30 × 100 = 79.1 consistency onset 23:50 vs usual 23:30 → 20 min off wake 07:20 vs usual 07:00 → 20 min off 100 × (1 − 20 ÷ 90) = 77.8 score 0.6 × 80.4 + 0.2 × 79.1 + 0.2 × 77.8 = 79.6 → 80

How far to trust it. The inputs are sound. The weights and cut-offs are design choices in the style of commercial trackers, not findings from a study. Sleep stages do not feed the score.

Background: adults need 7–9 hours (American Academy of Sleep Medicine); 8 is the middle.

Resting heart rate

src/health/metrics/heart-rate.ts

How slowly your heart beats when nothing is asked of it. A fitter heart pumps more blood per beat, so it needs fewer beats. Night to night, a resting rate above your normal is an early sign of stress, illness, alcohol or unfinished recovery.

How it is calculated

  1. Average heart rate per minute inside the sleep window. A minute counts only with 30 or more seconds of readings.
  2. Take the mean of every unbroken 5-minute run.
  3. Resting HR is the 10th percentile of those means.

Measuring during sleep removes posture, caffeine and mood. The 10th percentile finds one of your calmest stretches without trusting the single lowest reading, which is often a glitch.

Worked example

A night gives 90 five-minute means running from 49.8 to 63 bpm. Sorted, the value 10% of the way up the list is 52.1. Resting HR is 52.

How far to trust it. High. Heart rate at rest is what optical sensors measure best.

Heart rate variability

src/health/metrics/hrv.ts

The vagus nerve can slow the heart within a single beat. When it is in charge (rested, recovered, asleep), consecutive gaps differ more. When stress hormones dominate, the gaps even out. So, against intuition, more variability is the healthier sign. HRV is the most sensitive overnight signal of recovery, and also the noisiest.

How it is calculated

  1. Clean. Drop gaps outside 300–2,000 ms (200 to 30 bpm) and any gap more than 20% away from the median of its 11-beat neighbourhood. A missed or extra beat would otherwise look like huge variability.
  2. Successive differences. Subtract each gap from the next, only where both were kept and truly follow each other.
  3. Skip jumps. A difference is left out when a gap is more than 20% away from the gap before it. That is an ectopic (early or extra) beat or a misread one, not the vagus nerve. Cleaning against the median lets a lone short beat through, and those 0.9% of differences would add 1–5 ms to a night's RMSSD.
  4. RMSSD, the root mean square of successive differences, per 5-minute window:
    \text{RMSSD} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(RR_{i+1} - RR_i\right)^2}
  5. A window counts only if 80% of its beats survived cleaning and it has 60 or more.
  6. The night's HRV is the median window.
  7. Recovery uses \ln(\text{RMSSD}). HRV is skewed, and the logarithm turns equal ratios into equal steps, so a move from 30 to 45 ms counts the same as 60 to 90.

"Truly follow each other" takes some care. The strap stamps each packet of beats to the whole second, so a beat happened up to a second before its stamp, and its gaps add up about 2.4% short of the clock. The code therefore tracks the span of time each beat could have happened in: the last beat's span moved on by this gap, then cut to what this beat's stamp allows. Each stamp narrows the span. When it comes out empty, a beat must be missing, and no difference is taken across it. This keeps 84–98% of asleep beats, against 70–87% for the simpler rule it replaced.

Worked example

gaps (ms) 1000 1040 980 1010 990 differences +40 −60 +30 −20 (all under 20%) squared 1600 3600 900 400 sum 6500 RMSSD √(6500 ÷ 4) = 40.3 ms ln(RMSSD) 3.70

How far to trust it. The maths is the field's standard. The absolute value depends on the sensor and on where it is worn, so do not compare yours with anyone else's or with another device. Your own trend is what counts. Normal values range from under 20 ms to over 100 ms between healthy people.

Sources: Task Force 1996 (RMSSD and the 5-minute window, background); Malik 1989 (the 20% rule); Esco & Flatt 2014 and Munoz 2015 (a minute of beats is enough).

Breathing rate

src/health/metrics/breathing.ts

Breaths per minute during sleep. It is one of the steadiest numbers your body has: most people vary by less than one breath a minute from night to night. A clear rise is therefore informative, and often appears a day or two before you feel ill.

The strap has no breathing sensor. It reads breathing from the heart. Breathing in briefly releases the vagal brake and the heart speeds up; breathing out slows it. The beat gaps rise and fall once per breath. This is called respiratory sinus arrhythmia.

How it is calculated

  1. Take each good 5-minute HRV window. Resample the uneven beat gaps at an even 4 times a second.
  2. Remove the straight-line trend, so slow heart-rate drift does not swamp the wobble.
  3. Taper the ends (a Hann window) and take a Fourier transform, which splits the signal into its component rhythms and gives the strength of each.
  4. Keep the band from 0.1 to 0.5 Hz, which is 6 to 30 breaths a minute.
  5. Scale each window's spectrum to sum to 1 and average them over the night, so one noisy window cannot dominate.
  6. Take the tallest peak. It counts only if it is at least 2.5 times the band's median power.
\text{breaths per minute} = f_{\text{peak}} \times 60

Worked example

window used 270 s → frequency steps of 1 ÷ 270 Hz (0.22 breaths/min) tallest peak bin 69 → 69 ÷ 270 = 0.2556 Hz peak ÷ median 6.1 → over 2.5, so it counts breathing rate 0.2556 × 60 = 15.3 breaths/min

How far to trust it. Good for trends. The effect it relies on weakens with age and during REM, so some nights have no clear peak and no reading, which is better than a wrong one.

Source: Natarajan 2021 (Fitbit's method, validated against chest-belt breathing).

Skin temperature

src/health/metrics/skin-temp.ts

Asleep, the body sheds heat through the skin, so skin warms. Fever, alcohol, a hot room and the menstrual cycle all shift it. The absolute value depends on the room and the bedding, so only the change from your normal means much.

How it is calculated

  1. Skip the first 30 minutes of the sleep window while skin warms up.
  2. Take the median of the rest.
  3. Deviation = tonight minus the mean of the last 30 nights. It needs 7 nights first.

Worked example

tonight's median 34.6 °C 30-night mean 34.2 °C deviation +0.4 °C

How far to trust it. As a deviation, fair. As a body temperature, not at all: skin runs several degrees below core and follows the room.

Recovery

src/health/metrics/recovery.ts · src/health/params.ts

Recovery rolls the overnight signals into one percentage: how ready your body looks this morning compared with your own normal. It is not a measurement of anything physical. It is a summary of how far last night's readings sit from your baseline, and in which direction.

How it is calculated

  1. Baseline. For HRV, resting HR, breathing and sleep score, take the mean and standard deviation over the last 60 nights. It needs 14 nights before it says anything.
  2. z-score each of tonight's values against its baseline.
  3. Combine.
    c = 0.50\,z_{\text{HRV}} - 0.25\,z_{\text{RHR}} + 0.15\,z_{\text{sleep}} - 0.10\,\max(0,\ z_{\text{breathing}} - 1) - 0.5\,\max(0,\ \Delta T - 0.3)
    High HRV is good, so it adds. High resting HR is bad, so it subtracts. Breathing counts only past +1 standard deviation, and skin temperature only past +0.3 °C above normal: both are alarm signals, quiet on ordinary nights.
  4. Turn it into a percentage. Divide c by how much it usually varies for you (\sigma_c), then apply \Phi, the bell-curve function that gives the share of values below a point.
    \text{Recovery} = 100 \times \Phi\!\left(\frac{c}{\sigma_c}\right)

So recovery reads as "the share of your normal nights this one beats". An ordinary night lands near 50%. Bands: red under 34, yellow 34–66, green 67 and up.

Worked example: a good night

HRV ln RMSSD 3.80 vs mean 3.70, SD 0.20 z = +0.50 resting HR 51.2 vs mean 52, SD 2 z = −0.40 sleep 80 vs mean 77, SD 8 z = +0.375 breathing z = +0.4 (under +1, no penalty) skin temp +0.1 °C (under +0.3, no penalty) c = 0.50 × 0.50 − 0.25 × (−0.40) + 0.15 × 0.375 = 0.25 + 0.10 + 0.056 = 0.406 σc = 0.55 c ÷ σc = 0.74 Φ(0.74) = 0.77 Recovery = 77% (green)

And a bad one

HRV z −1.2, resting HR z +1.0, sleep z −1.0, breathing z +1.8, skin temp +0.7 °C c = −0.60 − 0.25 − 0.15 − 0.10 × 0.8 − 0.5 × 0.4 = −1.28 c ÷ σc = −2.33 Φ = 0.01 Recovery = 1% (red): the pattern of coming down with something

How far to trust it. The inputs are real signals with research behind them. The recipe (weights, thresholds, bands) is a design in the manner of Whoop, Oura and Garmin, none of which publish theirs. That is why the weights are tunable against how you actually feel.

Tuning recovery

src/health/feedback/refit.ts · backtest.ts · src/health/tune.ts

The weights 0.50, 0.25, 0.15 and 0.10 began as a guess. The feedback loop checks them against your morning check-ins, where you rate how you feel from 1 to 5.

  1. Feel is only ever the target. It never feeds recovery, so the score cannot simply mirror your mood.
  2. Re-fit. A ridge regression finds the weights that best predict feel from the four terms, while being pulled toward the current weights. With few mornings it stays close to what it has; with many it follows the data.
    w = \left(X^{\top}X + \lambda I\right)^{-1}\left(X^{\top}y + \lambda\, s\, w_{\text{current}}\right), \qquad \lambda = 30
    Here X holds each morning's terms, y the feel ratings, and s rescales the current weights into feel units.
  3. Guardrails. At least 30 rated mornings, no negative weights, the same total, and no weight moves by more than 0.10 at a time.
  4. Backtest. For each of the last 4 weeks, re-fit on the nights before it and score that week. New weights are proposed only if the rank correlation between recovery and feel rises by 0.05 or more and no day's score moves by more than 25 points.
  5. Each applied change gets a version number, and every stored row records which version made it.

Strain and zones

src/health/metrics/strain.ts · heart-rate.ts

Strain is how much work the day asked of your heart and circulation, on a scale from 0 to 21. It is built on TRIMP (training impulse), which adds up minutes weighted by how hard each one was.

How it is calculated

  1. Resting and max HR. Rest is last night's resting HR, with fallbacks to the past week. Max is hr_max_measured from your profile, else Tanaka's estimate:
    \text{HR}_{\text{max}} = 208 - 0.7 \times \text{age}
    For a 30-year-old that gives 187. The formula is an average: individuals differ from it by 10 bpm or more, so a measured max is better. With no birth date there is no strain.
  2. For each minute, work out heart-rate reserve. Minutes under 30% do not count: desk work and pottering about are not training.
  3. Each remaining minute adds:
    \text{TRIMP}_{\text{minute}} = \text{HRR} \times 0.64 \times e^{1.92 \times \text{HRR}}
    (For women the constants are 0.86 and 1.67.) The exponential is there because effort is not linear. Blood lactate, a marker of how hard muscles are working, climbs steeply as intensity rises, and the constants trace that curve. One minute at 90% of reserve counts about as much as seven at 35%.
  4. Squash onto 0–21.
    \text{Strain} = 21 \times \left(1 - e^{-\text{TRIMP}/100}\right)
    Each extra point is harder to earn than the last, and 21 can be approached but not reached.

Worked example

one minute at 150 bpm HRR = (150 − 52) ÷ 138 = 0.71 0.71 × 0.64 × e^(1.92 × 0.71) = 1.78 one minute at 100 bpm HRR = 0.35 → 0.43 one minute at 80 bpm HRR = 0.20 → 0 (under 0.3) 45-minute run at 150 45 × 1.78 = 80.0 40-minute walk at 100 40 × 0.43 = 17.4 day's TRIMP 97.3 Strain = 21 × (1 − e^(−0.973)) = 13.1

Heart-rate zones

Minutes spent in five bands of maximum heart rate (not reserve). For a max of 190:

Zone% of maxFor max 190Feels like
150–6095–113Easy walking
260–70114–132Can hold a conversation
370–80133–151Steady, short sentences
480–90152–170Hard, a few words
590+171+All out

How far to trust it. TRIMP is well established for steady cardio. It undercounts lifting and sprints, where muscles work hard but the heart lags. The 0–21 scale copies Whoop's feel and has no meaning beyond "more is more". Everything rests on resting and max HR being right.

Sources: Banister 1991 (TRIMP); Tanaka 2001 (max HR from age).

Training load

src/health/metrics/load.ts

Strain describes one day. Training load asks whether your recent week is more or less than you are used to. Fitness builds when you do a little more than your body is adapted to. Doing a lot more, suddenly, is how people get hurt or run down.

How it is calculated

  1. Start from each day's TRIMP. (Not strain: strain is squashed, so it cannot be averaged.)
  2. Acute load is an exponentially weighted average over about 7 days. Chronic load is the same over about 28 days. Each day the average moves a fraction \lambda of the way toward today's value:
    \text{EWMA}_{\text{today}} = \lambda \times \text{TRIMP}_{\text{today}} + (1 - \lambda) \times \text{EWMA}_{\text{yesterday}}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{2}{N + 1}
    So \lambda is 0.25 for acute and 0.069 for chronic. Recent days weigh more, and a hard day fades out smoothly instead of dropping off a cliff on day 8.
  3. Ratio = acute ÷ chronic.
RatioLabelMeaning
Under 0.8Easing offDoing less than you are used to
0.8–1.3SteadyRoughly your usual, or building gently
1.3–1.5Building fastClearly more than usual
Over 1.5SpikeFar more than usual

Missing days are filled. A day with no data, or with the strap worn under 16 hours, gets the median TRIMP of the well-worn days in the 28 before. Today is a running total, so it counts as at least that median until the day ends. Otherwise the ratio would sag every morning. There is no acute load with more than 2 filled days in the last 7, and no chronic load under 28 days of history.

Worked example

seven weeks at TRIMP 60 a day, then one week at 100 a day acute 100 − 40 × 0.75^7 = 94.7 chronic 100 − 40 × (27/29)^7 = 75.7 ratio 94.7 ÷ 75.7 = 1.25 → steady the same with a week at 130 a day ratio 120.7 ÷ 87.6 = 1.38 → building fast

How far to trust it. As a description of "more or less than usual", it is sound. The bands come from Gabbett's work linking the ratio to injury in team sports, and that link is disputed: Impellizzeri and colleagues argue the ratio has statistical flaws and does not predict injury. That is why the note says "load" and never "risk".

Sources: Williams 2017 (exponential weighting); Gabbett 2016 (the bands); Impellizzeri 2020 (the critique).

Calories

src/health/metrics/calories.ts

The energy your body used in the day, in kilocalories. Most of it is not exercise. It is the cost of staying alive, called the basal metabolic rate (BMR): heart, brain, liver, body heat. Movement adds to that.

The day is priced minute by minute and summed.

Step 1: the base rate

BMR comes from the Mifflin–St Jeor equation, in kcal per day:

\text{BMR} = 10 \times \text{kg} + 6.25 \times \text{cm} - 5 \times \text{age} + 5 \quad (\text{women: } -161)

It is spread evenly over the day's 1,440 minutes.

Step 2: price each minute

MinuteRateWhy
AsleepBMR × 0.95Sleeping metabolism runs about 5% under BMR
Strap not wornBMR × 1.2The usual "sedentary" multiplier: awake and sitting about
Awake and wornThe largest of sitting, walking and heart rateEach method is good in a different range

Sitting is BMR × 1.2.

Walking uses steps, for minutes with 60 or more. Step length is taken as 0.415 × height. Speed is steps × step length, capped at 100 m/min. The ACSM walking equation gives the oxygen cost, and each litre of oxygen burned releases about 5 kcal.

\dot{V}O_2 = 3.5 + 0.1 \times \text{speed (m/min)} \quad \text{ml of oxygen per kg per minute}

The 3.5 is the oxygen cost of resting, known as 1 MET.

Heart rate uses Keytel's equation, fitted on people exercising while their oxygen use was measured. In kJ per minute, for men:

E = -55.0969 + 0.6309 \times \text{HR} + 0.1988 \times \text{kg} + 0.2017 \times \text{age}

(Women: −20.4022 + 0.4472 × HR − 0.1263 × kg + 0.074 × age.) Divide by 4.184 for kcal.

Heart rate only tracks energy use once you are working. At low rates it rises with coffee, heat and nerves. So it is phased in: no weight below 40% of heart-rate reserve (the "flex point"), full weight at 60%, and a straight ramp between.

\text{HR kcal} = \text{sitting} + w \times \max(0,\ \text{Keytel} - \text{sitting}), \qquad w = \frac{\text{HRR} - 0.4}{0.6 - 0.4} \text{ kept within } 0 \text{ to } 1

Step 3: total and active

Total is the sum of all minutes. Active calories are the total minus plain BMR for the same minutes, never below zero.

Worked example

BMR 10 × 70 + 6.25 × 175 − 5 × 30 + 5 = 1,649 kcal/day per minute 1.145 asleep 1.088 sitting 1.374 walking minute, 110 steps, HR 100 step length 0.415 × 1.75 m = 0.726 m speed 110 × 0.726 = 79.9 m/min oxygen 3.5 + 0.1 × 79.9 = 11.49 ml/kg/min kcal 11.49 × 70 ÷ 1000 × 5 = 4.02 heart rate HRR 0.35, under the flex point → adds nothing minute 4.02 kcal (walking wins) running minute, HR 150 Keytel (−55.10 + 94.64 + 13.92 + 6.05) ÷ 4.184 = 14.22 HRR 0.71, over 0.6 → full weight minute 14.22 kcal (heart rate wins) in-between minute, HR 114 HRR 0.449 w = (0.449 − 0.4) ÷ 0.2 = 0.246 Keytel 8.79 minute 1.374 + 0.246 × (8.79 − 1.374) = 3.20 kcal the whole day 450 min asleep × 1.088 = 489 45 min run × 14.22 = 640 40 min walk × 4.02 = 161 905 min sitting × 1.374 = 1,243 total 2,534 kcal active 2,534 − 1,649 = 885 kcal

How far to trust it. Expect errors of 10–20% on the day's total and more on single workouts. BMR equations are within about 10% for most people. Keytel's equation probably reads high for light people: it gives 13.4 kcal/min at 150 bpm for a 56 kg man where standard tables suggest about 9 for a steady jog. No correction is applied because the paper's data does not support one. Walking calories lean on the step counter, whose meaning is inferred. Without weight, height, birth date, sex, or resting and max HR, there are no calories at all, which beats a confident wrong total.

Sources: Mifflin 1990 (BMR); Keytel 2005 (energy from heart rate); Spurr 1988 (the flex point); ACSM guidelines (walking equation); Tudor-Locke 2018 (cadence).

VO2max

src/health/metrics/vo2max.ts

VO2max is the most oxygen your body can take in and use per minute, per kilogram of body weight, at full effort. It is the standard measure of aerobic fitness and one of the strongest single predictors of long-term health. A lab measures it with a mask on a treadmill run to exhaustion. Here it is estimated from two heart rates.

How it is calculated

\dot{V}O_{2\text{max}} = 15.3 \times \frac{\text{HR}_{\text{max}}}{\text{HR}_{\text{rest}}} \quad \text{ml/kg/min} \qquad (\text{women: } 14.5)

Why would a ratio of heart rates say anything about oxygen? Oxygen uptake is heart rate × blood pumped per beat × oxygen extracted from that blood. At rest everyone needs about the same oxygen per kilogram. A fit heart pumps more per beat, so it meets that need with fewer beats. The lower your resting rate is against your max, the more headroom you have.

  • Resting HR is the median of the last 30 nights, so the number moves slowly, as fitness does. It needs 14 nights with a reading.
  • Max HR must be one you have actually seen (hr_max_measured). With an age-predicted max, the result would just be age and resting HR dressed up.

Worked example

15.3 × 190 ÷ 52 = 55.9 → shown as "56 (estimate)" if resting HR were 60 48.5 if resting HR were 45 64.6

Read 56 as "somewhere between 48 and 64".

How far to trust it. Loosely: ±10–15%. The formula's error was 2.7 ml/kg/min in the 46 well-trained men it was built on, about 7 in 109 men of mixed fitness, and it read about 2 too high in untrained men. A naturally high max heart rate raises the number without any extra fitness. Use it to watch your own direction over months. A falling resting heart rate will show as a rising VO2max.

Sources: Uth 2004 (the method); Uth 2005 (the factor for women); Esco 2012 and Castagna 2022 (how it does outside trained men).

Steps and walks

src/health/metrics/steps.ts · walks.ts

Steps

The strap reports a running counter that goes up to 65,535 and then wraps to zero. Steps are the sum of its increases. A drop from near the top is a wrap; a drop from lower down is a restart. Jumps of more than 5 steps a second are ignored as glitches.

Worked example

counter reads 65,530, then 12 wrap: 12 − 65,530 + 65,536 = 18 steps

Walks

A walk rarely lifts heart rate enough to count as a workout, so it is found from steps alone.

  1. A walking minute has 60 or more steps.
  2. Walking minutes join across stops of up to 3 minutes (crossings, lights).
  3. A walk is 10 minutes or longer, outside sleep and workouts. Five minutes is enough right before or after a recorded run.
  4. Each walk records its steps, its median cadence, and its brisk minutes: those at 100 or more steps a minute, which is about 3 METs, the line for "moderate intensity" in public-health guidelines.

How far to trust it. Provisional. What the counter field means was inferred, not documented by the maker.

Source: Tudor-Locke 2018 (100 steps a minute as moderate intensity).

Workouts and lifting

src/health/metrics/workouts.ts · runs.ts · sets.ts · src/health/lifting.ts

Nothing here is a physiological measurement. These are detection rules, tuned by hand on a few sessions, that find when you exercised and guess what it was.

Finding a workout

  1. Baseline: the 25th percentile of the day's awake heart rate.
  2. Active minute: 20 or more bpm above baseline, or 8 or more with clear motion, or a big heart-rate climb peaks in it. Active minutes join across rests of up to 5 minutes.
  3. Big climb: a rise of 25 or more bpm from the lowest point in the previous 90 seconds. A lifting set typically produces 30–58.
  4. Keep stretches of 15 minutes or more that have real motion (a still strap with high heart rate is an optical glitch) and are either hard (5+ minutes at 50% of reserve) or spiking and calming (6+ climbs, at least 2 per 10 minutes).

Naming it

NameRule, checked in this order
RunningHalf the minutes at 140+ steps a minute
WalkingHalf at 90+ steps
Volleyball30%+ of minutes at 20–89 steps and 20%+ with strong motion
LiftingSpiking and calming, 60%+ of minutes under 20 steps
WorkoutAnything else

Each workout gets its own TRIMP, strain and calories, using the same per-minute maths as the day. A run recorded on Strava replaces any detected workout it overlaps and is marked confirmed, with its distance. Uncertain names are marked "possible" and asked about on the Activity page.

Matching lifting sets to heart rate

If you log sets in the daily note, each is matched to the heart-rate climb it caused. The code smooths heart rate over 15 seconds, finds each peak's climb from the lowest point in the previous 90 seconds, and gives the N clearest climbs, at least a minute apart, to the N logged sets in order. With fewer clear climbs than sets, nothing is matched. Volume is weight × reps.

How far to trust it. Start and end times are usually close. Names are provisional: the rules were checked against only a few sessions, and no runs before Strava was connected.
